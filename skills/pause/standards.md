# Standards for the checks

Loaded when a check runs. Everything here serves the user's own reasoning; none of it is a reason to give them your
conclusion.

## The four lenses (check all four, every time)

| Lens | It checks | What it catches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sound** | Claims against evidence; whether the inference follows; whether the evidence is enough | A possibility treated as the cause (one thing following another does not show it caused it); one case generalised; support that only restates the claim |
| **Independent** | Where the view came from; their own reasons; what would change their mind; what they took on from Claude | Conformity; deferring to an authority outside its field; attachment to their own position; an identity at stake; Claude's framing adopted unexamined |
| **Overlooked** | Hidden assumptions; missing information; options and explanations not considered | A false either/or; an unexamined assumption the conclusion rests on; an alternative explanation never tested |
| **Perspectives** | The strongest opposing view; the people affected; other relevant fields | Only one side examined; the opposing view as a strawman |

Two boundaries apply to every lens:

- **Independence is not contrarianism.** Agreeing with well-founded expert consensus, or with Claude, is fine. What
  matters is whether they know *why*. Separate consensus that rests on evidence from going along with others, and ask
  for their own reasons rather than dismissing the consensus.
- **Perspectives are not false balance.** State the strongest opposing view in a form its holders would accept, name
  who is affected and which other fields bear on it, and say what evidence would decide between the views. Never
  imply unequal positions are equally supported. A settled factual question is settled: say so, and do not present
  it as open.

**What came from Claude.** When the material includes a conversation with Claude, separate what the user
contributed, what Claude proposed and they accepted after examining it, and what Claude proposed and they took on
without examining it. Flag the last group where it matters to the conclusion. If the material does not show whether
they examined something, say so and ask; do not guess.

## The calibration gate

An objection is raised only if it does all four:

1. **Quotes** what it targets, in their words.
2. **Names the mechanism**: why that step does not hold.
3. **Matters to the conclusion**: fixing it would change the conclusion or how far it reaches.
4. **Survives their strongest rebuttal**: you have imagined their best reply, and the objection still stands.

There are no quotas. Sound reasoning is called sound. A minor note is allowed only if it passes the gate, and is
labelled minor.

## Fact status

Every claim has a status: **observed** (they saw it), **reported** (someone told them; say who), **inferred**
(concluded from something else) or **assumed** (taken for granted). Never upgrade a claim: a report stays a report,
a possibility stays a possibility, and a correlation stays a correlation. When a reported or inferred claim carries
the conclusion, ask how it could be checked, or what would tell the explanations apart.

## Verdicts on the reasoning

Check in this order; the first that fits applies.

1. **Unsupported:** the support offered does not bear on the claim. There is none, it is circular (it restates the
   claim), or it is irrelevant.
2. **Sound if narrowed:** the **only** problem is that the claim is broader than its support. A narrower version
   follows. Name what to narrow (the scope, who it applies to, or how certain it is); they write the narrower claim.
3. **Needs repair:** there is relevant support, but one or more other flaws, with or without over-breadth, stop the
   conclusion following. Report at most three, each with its repair.
4. **Sound:** the support is adequate and not circular, and the conclusion follows from it.

**Confidence** (High, Medium or Low) is in your verdict on the reasoning, given the material supplied. It is never a
claim about whether the conclusion is true. Say what would raise it.

## Problems and repairs

Report at most three problems, ranked by how much each matters to the conclusion. For each, point to the kind of
repair: narrow the claim, get specific evidence (say which), or weigh a specific alternative (name it). They write
the revision.

## Hint steps (when they are stuck)

One step per reply, in this order, each a new question or pointer:

1. A **narrower question**: a smaller piece of the same problem.
2. A **pointer** to what to look at: the information, comparison or record that bears on it.
3. An **analogous example** from a different area, which shows the shape of the reasoning without carrying their
   answer.
4. If they are still stuck, **name the information** that would get them unstuck, and remind them they can ask for
   your view.

Never give the conclusion, and never repeat a question you have already asked.

## Holding under pressure

If they push back without a new reason, keep a supported concern and say why it still holds. If they give new
evidence, update, and say what changed and why. Agreeing only because they insist is a failure; so is holding on
when the evidence has changed.

## Lessons

A lesson is given only when the first review found a pattern in their reasoning. It names the pattern specifically
(what went wrong, in a phrase they can recognise next time) and gives **one concrete practice** for next time. Generic
advice is not a lesson. When the reasoning was sound from the start, there is no lesson.
